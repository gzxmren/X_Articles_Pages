The Problem: Why Traditional Time Series Models Fail at Scale

Before we dissect foundation models, we need to understand why the previous generation failed. Classical time series forecasting - ARIMA, exponential smoothing, state-space models - rests on a set of assumptions that financial data systematically violates.
Non-stationarity. ARIMA(p,d,q) models require differencing the series d times to achieve stationarity, where the augmented Dickey-Fuller test confirms that E[y_t] and Var(y_t) are time-invariant. But financial returns exhibit structural breaks: the 2008 crisis, the COVID crash, the 2022 rate hiking cycle. Each break shifts the data-generating process. A model trained on pre-2022 data will mispredict post-2022 inflation dynamics because the parameters φ_i and θ_j in the ARIMA specification are no longer valid.
The cold-start problem. Every classical model must be trained from scratch on each new series. If you have 5,000 stocks to forecast, you fit 5,000 separate models. This is computationally wasteful and statistically inefficient - the model for Apple learns nothing from the model for Microsoft, even though their price dynamics are correlated.
Feature engineering burden. Classical approaches require manual specification of seasonality, trend decomposition, and exogenous regressors. For a single equity forecast, a quant might specify calendar effects (month-end, earnings windows), market microstructure features (order flow, bid-ask spread), and macroeconomic indicators (yield curve slope, VIX). Each feature must be engineered, tested for significance, and monitored for decay.
The training cost problem. Deep learning alternatives - LSTMs, Transformers applied to individual series - require substantial data per series. A 10-year daily history provides only ~2,500 observations. Transformer architectures overfit aggressively at this sample size unless the model is tiny, which limits expressiveness.
Foundation models promise to solve all four problems simultaneously. They are pretrained on massive, diverse time series corpora (billions of observations across domains), then deployed with minimal or zero fine-tuning on the target task. The pretraining corpus encodes generic temporal patterns - trend, seasonality, autocorrelation structures - that transfer across domains. Apple's seasonality partially transfers to Microsoft. Weather temperature cycles partially transfer to energy demand patterns, which partially transfer to energy equity price dynamics.
But this transfer is the crux of the problem. How much transfers? What is lost? And what happens when the target domain is financial markets - the most adversarial prediction environment on Earth?

The Theory: How Time Series Foundation Models Work

To understand why TSFMs differ from each other, we need to understand the mathematical pipeline they all share, and the architectural decisions that distinguish them.
From Language to Time Series: The Tokenization Problem
Large language models operate on discrete tokens - words or subwords mapped to integers via a vocabulary. Time series are continuous. The first architectural decision every TSFM makes is: how do we tokenize a continuous sequence x = (x_1, x_2, ..., x_T) into a discrete representation suitable for a transformer?
There are three distinct approaches in the literature, and they define the major model families.
Approach 1: Scalar Tokenization via Quantization (Chronos).
Ansari et al. (2024) propose a surprisingly simple strategy: scale the time series to a fixed range, then quantize into bins. The pipeline is:
Scale: Transform the input series by
x~t=(xt−mean(x))/std(x)⋅Sx̃_t = (x_t − mean(x)) / std(x) · S
where S is a scaling constant (typically S = 100).
Quantize : Map each scaled value to one of V bins via uniform quantization:
token(x~t)=⌊(x~t−xmin)/Δ⌋,Δ=(xmax−xmin)/Vtoken(x̃_t) = ⌊(x̃_t − x_min) / Δ⌋, Δ = (x_max − x_min) / V
where V is the vocabulary size (Chronos uses V = 4096) and additional reserved tokens handle special cases (padding, end-of-sequence, values outside the range).
Model: Feed the token sequence into a standard causal language model (Chronos uses a T5 encoder-decoder architecture). The loss is standard cross-entropy over the vocabulary:
L=−Σt=1TΣv=1V𝟙[tokent=v]⋅logPθ(v∣token<t)L = − Σ_{t=1}^{T} Σ_{v=1}^{V} 𝟙[token_t = v] · log P_θ(v | token_{<t})
At inference time, the model generates token sequences autoregressively, which are then de-tokenized (mapped back to continuous values) by taking the bin center or sampling from the predicted categorical distribution.
The elegance of this approach is that it repurposes the entire language modeling stack - token embeddings, positional encodings, causal masking, beam search decoding - with minimal modification. Chronos models range from 8M to 710M parameters, and the largest variant outperforms task-specific models on 42 benchmark datasets in zero-shot evaluation.
Approach 2: Patch Projection (MOIRAI).
Woo et al. (2024) take a fundamentally different path. Rather than discretizing values, they group consecutive time steps into patches and project each patch to a continuous vector via a learned linear transformation.
Given a time series x ∈ ℝ^T and patch size P, the patching operation creates a sequence of patch vectors:
zi=W⋅xiP:(i+1)P+b,i=0,1,...,⌊T/P⌋−1z_i = W · x_{iP:(i+1)P} + b, i = 0, 1, ..., ⌊T/P⌋ − 1
where W ∈ ℝ^{d_model × P} and b ∈ ℝ^{d_model} are learned parameters. The sequence of patch embeddings Z = (z_0, z_1, ...) is then processed by a transformer encoder.
MOIRAI introduces Any-variate Attention, a key innovation that allows the model to handle multivariate series with varying numbers of covariates without architectural changes. Standard transformers assume a fixed input dimension; MOIRAI uses a variate-specific embedding that encodes which time series each patch belongs to, and a specialized attention mask that prevents information leakage between variates at the same timestep. The attention mechanism is:
Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKT/√dk+M)VAttention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / √d_k + M) V
where M is a mask that enforces causal structure both temporally (no looking ahead) and cross-sectionally (appropriate handling of multiple variates).
MOIRAI is trained on LOTSA - the Large Open Time Series Archive - comprising 27 billion observations across 9 domains. The model uses masked prediction (similar to BERT in NLP): random patches are masked, and the model learns to reconstruct them. The loss is:
L=Σi∈M∣xiP:(i+1)P−x^iP:(i+1)P∣2L = Σ_{i ∈ M} |x_{iP:(i+1)P} − x̂_{iP:(i+1)P}|²
where M is the set of masked patch indices and x̂ is the decoder reconstruction.
MOIRAI comes in sizes from 14M to 311M parameters, with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) variant (Woo et al., 2024) that achieves comparable performance with 30x fewer active parameters and 2x faster inference.
Approach 3: Binary Spherical Quantization (Kronos).
Shi et al. (2025) recognized that general TSFMs systematically underperform on financial data. Their diagnosis: financial time series have fundamentally different statistical properties - fat-tailed distributions, volatility clustering, extreme events - that general-domain pretraining does not capture. Their solution is Kronos, the first TSFM pretrained exclusively on financial data, with a tokenization strategy tailored to financial K-line (candlestick) data.
Kronos uses Binary Spherical Quantization (BSQ). The idea is to represent each K-line observation (Open, High, Low, Close, Volume) as a vector, normalize it to the unit sphere, then quantize it into binary codes. The pipeline is:
Vectorize: Each K-line becomes a feature vector v = (O, H, L, C, V) ∈ ℝ^5 (or higher-dimensional variants with additional technical indicators).
Spherical normalize: Project onto the unit sphere:
v^=v/‖v‖2v̂ = v / ‖v‖₂
Binary quantize: Map to a binary code using learned prototypes:
BSQ(v^)=argminc∈C‖v^−c‖2BSQ(v̂) = argmin_{c ∈ C} ‖v̂ − c‖²
where C is a codebook of centroids on the unit sphere, learned via a variant of spherical k-means. The resulting binary representation is then embedded into a continuous space for the transformer.
BSQ has two advantages for financial data. First, spherical normalization is invariant to scale changes - a 1% move in a $10 stock and a 1% move in a $1000 stock map to the same region of the sphere. This invariance is crucial because price levels are arbitrary (splits, denominational differences across exchanges) while returns distributions have structural regularities. Second, binary quantization creates a compact, information-theoretically efficient representation that captures the discrete nature of price movements (tick-level data is inherently granular).
Kronos was trained on over 12 billion K-line records across 45 global exchanges, making it by far the largest financial-specific pretraining corpus. Models range from 4.1M to 499M parameters.
The Shared Transformer Backbone
Despite their different tokenization strategies, all four models use a transformer backbone. The core computation is the multi-head self-attention mechanism:
MultiHead(X)=Concat(head1,...,headh)WOMultiHead(X) = Concat(head_1, ..., head_h) W^O
where each head computes:
headi=Attention(XWiQ,XWiK,XWiV)head_i = Attention(X W_i^Q, X W_i^K, X W_i^V)
Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKT/√dk)VAttention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / √d_k) V
with causal masking for autoregressive models (Chronos) and bidirectional attention for encoder-only models (MOIRAI, Kronos). TimeGPT uses a proprietary architecture not fully disclosed but follows the same transformer paradigm with specialized positional encodings for temporal data.
The feed-forward sublayer applies:
FFN(x)=GELU(xW1+b1)W2+b2FFN(x) = GELU(x W_1 + b_1) W_2 + b_2
with layer normalization and residual connections at each sublayer:
xout=LayerNorm(x+Sublayer(x))x_out = LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))

TimeGPT: The First Mover
Garza and Mergenthaler-Canseco (2023) released TimeGPT-1 as the first production-ready time series foundation model. It was trained on over 100 billion data points spanning multiple domains and offers an API-first deployment model with 0.6ms inference latency. The architecture has not been fully disclosed - Nixtla treats it as proprietary - but it is known to use a transformer backbone with custom positional encodings and normalization schemes designed for heterogeneous time series. TimeGPT supports both univariate and multivariate forecasting, anomaly detection, and cross-sectional learning. Its primary limitation is the lack of transparency: practitioners cannot inspect, modify, or self-host the model, making it unsuitable for trading firms with strict data sovereignty requirements.
Chronos: Language Models for Time Series
Ansari et al. (2024) demonstrated that language model architectures can be repurposed for time series with minimal modification. Their key insight: by quantizing continuous values into a vocabulary, time series forecasting becomes a token prediction problem - the exact problem language models solve. They pretrained T5-based models of varying sizes (Tiny: 8M, Mini: 20M, Small: 46M, Base: 200M, Large: 710M parameters) on a corpus of publicly available time series datasets augmented with synthetic data generated via Gaussian processes and ARIMA models.
Chronos achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot performance on 42 benchmark datasets, often matching or exceeding task-specific deep learning models that were trained on each individual dataset. The 710M Large model showed the strongest performance, confirming the scaling hypothesis: larger models pretrained on diverse data generalize better.
Ansari et al. (2025) extended Chronos to Chronos-2, adding multivariate support and covariate-informed prediction. Chronos-2 can condition on external variables (holidays, promotions, economic indicators) and model dependencies between multiple time series. The model has been downloaded over 600,000 times from HuggingFace. Chronos-Bolt - a subsequent optimized variant - achieves 5% lower error while being 250x faster and 20x more memory efficient, making it viable for production forecasting pipelines.
The Chronos tokenization pipeline (scale + quantize + embed + predict + dequantize) is a critical design decision. It enables the reuse of the entire language modeling stack but introduces approximation error at both the quantization and dequantization steps. For financial applications, this error matters: a small shift in predicted volatility can dramatically change option pricing.
MOIRAI: Universal Time Series Forecasting
Woo et al. (2024) built MOIRAI around a different philosophy: instead of converting time series to tokens, keep them in continuous space and use patch-level processing. The Any-variate Attention mechanism is the architectural centerpiece. Standard multivariate models require a fixed number of input series. MOIRAI handles any number of variates dynamically - you can train on a dataset with 10 series and deploy on a dataset with 500 series without architectural changes.
The model was trained on LOTSA, the largest open time series collection to date: 27 billion observations across 9 domains (energy, transport, healthcare, weather, web traffic, finance, and others). The masked prediction objective encourages the model to learn robust representations that capture the full distribution of temporal patterns.
The MoE variant (Woo et al., 2024) replaces the dense feed-forward layers with sparse expert layers. At each layer, a gating network routes each token to a small subset of experts. This achieves comparable forecasting accuracy with dramatically reduced inference cost - 30x fewer active parameters, 2x faster throughput.
MOIRAI's authors acknowledge a critical limitation: the model relies on "heuristic cross-frequency learning." There is no explicit mechanism to align or relate patterns at different temporal frequencies (daily, weekly, monthly). The model must learn these relationships implicitly from data, which may not always succeed - especially for financial data where multi-scale dynamics (intraday microstructure, daily momentum, monthly seasonality) are critical.
Kronos: Finance-First Foundation Model
Shi et al. (2025) is the paper that financial quants have been waiting for. Kronos is the first time series foundation model pretrained exclusively on financial market data - over 12 billion K-line records from 45 global exchanges. The model was evaluated on a comprehensive suite of financial tasks: volatility forecasting, return prediction, synthetic data generation, and portfolio construction.
The results are striking. Kronos outperformed all 25 baseline models on financial tasks, including general TSFMs (TimesFM, Chronos) and domain-specific baselines. On volatility forecasting, Kronos achieved 9% lower MAE than the best general-purpose alternative. On synthetic K-line generation, it improved fidelity by 22%. Most importantly for trading applications, Kronos achieved a RankIC (Rank Information Coefficient) 93% higher than general TSFMs - meaning its predicted return rankings are substantially more correlated with actual future returns.
The key differentiator is not the architecture (Kronos uses a standard transformer) but the data and tokenization. By pretraining exclusively on financial K-line data, Kronos learns the specific statistical properties of markets: fat-tailed return distributions, volatility clustering (GARCH-like dynamics), asymmetric momentum, and cross-asset correlation structures. BSQ tokenization preserves scale invariance and handles the discrete granularity of tick data.
Kronos has gained significant traction: 16,400 GitHub stars and an MIT license, making it fully deployable in proprietary trading systems without licensing restrictions.

from chronos import ChronosPipeline

def load_chronos_model(model_size="small"):
    """
    Load a pre-trained Chronos model from HuggingFace.
    Available sizes: "tiny" (8M), "mini" (20M), "small" (46M),
    "base" (200M), "large" (710M)
    """
    model_name = f"amazon/chronos-t5-{model_size}-2024-07"
    pipeline = ChronosPipeline.from_pretrained(
        model_name,
        device_map="auto",
        torch_dtype=torch.float32,
    )
    return pipeline


def chronos_forecast(pipeline, context_series, prediction_length, num_samples=100):
    """
    Generate probabilistic forecasts using Chronos.

    Parameters:
    -----------
    pipeline : ChronosPipeline
        Loaded Chronos model
    context_series : np.ndarray
        Historical time series values, shape (T,)
    prediction_length : int
        Number of steps to forecast
    num_samples : int
        Number of sample paths to generate

    Returns:
    --------
    forecasts : np.ndarray
        Sample paths of shape (num_samples, prediction_length)
    """
    context = torch.tensor(context_series, dtype=torch.float32)
    if context.ndim == 1:
        context = context.unsqueeze(0)

    forecasts = pipeline.predict(
        context,
        prediction_length,
        num_samples=num_samples,
        temperature=1.0,
        top_k=50,
        top_p=1.0,
    )

    return forecasts.numpy()


# ---- Example: Zero-shot stock price forecasting ----
if __name__ == "__main__":
    np.random.seed(42)
    T = 500
    returns = np.random.randn(T) * 0.02 + 0.0005
    prices = 100 * np.exp(np.cumsum(returns))

    context_length = 252
    prediction_length = 21

    context = prices[-context_length:]

    print("Loading Chronos model...")
    pipeline = load_chronos_model("small")

    print("Generating forecasts...")
    forecasts = chronos_forecast(
        pipeline, context, prediction_length, num_samples=100
    )

    point_forecast = np.median(forecasts[0], axis=0)
    lower_95 = np.percentile(forecasts[0], 2.5, axis=0)
    upper_95 = np.percentile(forecasts[0], 97.5, axis=0)

    print(f"\nContext (last 5 values): {context[-5:]}")
    print(f"Point forecast (next {prediction_length} days): {point_forecast}")
    print(f"95% CI lower bound: {lower_95}")
    print(f"95% CI upper bound: {upper_95}")

    split_point = len(prices) - prediction_length
    train = prices[:split_point]
    test = prices[split_point:]

    val_forecasts = chronos_forecast(
        pipeline, train[-context_length:], prediction_length, num_samples=50
    )
    val_point = np.median(val_forecasts[0], axis=0)

    actual_directions = np.sign(np.diff(test))
    predicted_directions = np.sign(np.diff(val_point))
    directional_accuracy = np.mean(actual_directions == predicted_directions)
    print(f"\nDirectional accuracy: {directional_accuracy:.2%}")
    print(f"Test RMSE: {np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(test, val_point)):.4f}")

from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

def load_kronos_model(model_size="base"):
    """
    Load Kronos model from HuggingFace.
    Kronos sizes: "micro" (4.1M), "tiny" (17M), "mini" (38M),
    "small" (78M), "medium" (151M), "large" (311M), "xl" (499M)
    """
    model_name = f"shiyu-coder/Kronos-{model_size}"
    tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(
        model_name, trust_remote_code=True
    )
    model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
        model_name,
        trust_remote_code=True,
        torch_dtype=torch.float32,
    ).to(DEVICE)
    return model, tokenizer


def kronos_encode_kline(open_p, high_p, low_p, close_p, volume,
                        tokenizer, return_tensors="pt"):
    """
    Encode a K-line (OHLCV) tuple for Kronos input.
    Kronos expects specific formatting for K-line data.
    """
    kline_str = f"O:{open_p:.4f} H:{high_p:.4f} L:{low_p:.4f} " \
                f"C:{close_p:.4f} V:{volume:.2f}"
    tokens = tokenizer(kline_str, return_tensors=return_tensors)
    return tokens


def kronos_predict_returns(model, tokenizer, kline_history,
                           prediction_horizon=5):
    """
    Predict future returns from K-line history using Kronos.

    Parameters:
    -----------
    model : Kronos model
    tokenizer : Kronos tokenizer
    kline_history : list of tuples
        List of (O, H, L, C, V) K-line records
    prediction_horizon : int
        Number of future periods to predict

    Returns:
    --------
    predicted_returns : np.ndarray
        Predicted log returns for each future period
    """
    model.eval()

    kline_texts = []
    for o, h, l, c, v in kline_history:
        kline_texts.append(
            f"O:{o:.4f} H:{h:.4f} L:{l:.4f} C:{c:.4f} V:{v:.2f}"
        )

    prompt = "[HISTORY] " + " | ".join(kline_texts) + " [PREDICT]"
    inputs = tokenizer(prompt, return_tensors="pt").to(DEVICE)

    with torch.no_grad():
        outputs = model.generate(
            **inputs,
            max_new_tokens=prediction_horizon * 10,
            do_sample=True,
            temperature=0.8,
            top_p=0.9,
            num_return_sequences=1,
        )

    decoded = tokenizer.decode(outputs[0], skip_special_tokens=True)

    import re
    return_pattern = r'R:([+-]?\d+\.\d+)'
    matches = re.findall(return_pattern, decoded)
    predicted_returns = np.array([float(m) for m in matches[:prediction_horizon]])

    if len(predicted_returns) < prediction_horizon:
        padding = np.zeros(prediction_horizon - len(predicted_returns))
        predicted_returns = np.concatenate([predicted_returns, padding])

    return predicted_returns

Part 3: Fine-Tuning for Returns Prediction

import torch.nn as nn
from torch.utils.data import Dataset, DataLoader

class ReturnsDataset(Dataset):
    """
    Dataset for fine-tuning a TSFM on returns prediction.
    Creates (features, target_return) pairs from price series.
    """
    def __init__(self, prices, lookback_window=60, horizon=5):
        self.prices = prices
        self.lookback = lookback_window
        self.horizon = horizon

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self.prices) - self.lookback - self.horizon

    def __getitem__(self, idx):
        window = self.prices[idx:idx + self.lookback]
        log_returns = np.diff(np.log(window + 1e-8))

        mean = np.mean(log_returns)
        std = np.std(log_returns) + 1e-8
        features = (log_returns - mean) / std

        future_prices = self.prices[
            idx + self.lookback:idx + self.lookback + self.horizon
        ]
        target = np.log(future_prices[-1] / window[-1])

        return (
            torch.tensor(features, dtype=torch.float32),
            torch.tensor([target], dtype=torch.float32)
        )


class SimpleTSFM(nn.Module):
    """
    Lightweight transformer for returns prediction.
    Designed for fine-tuning on financial data.
    """
    def __init__(self, input_dim=1, d_model=64, nhead=4,
                 num_layers=3, dim_feedforward=256, dropout=0.1):
        super().__init__()
        self.input_proj = nn.Linear(input_dim, d_model)
        encoder_layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(
            d_model=d_model,
            nhead=nhead,
            dim_feedforward=dim_feedforward,
            dropout=dropout,
            batch_first=True,
        )
        self.transformer = nn.TransformerEncoder(
            encoder_layer, num_layers=num_layers
        )
        self.output_proj = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(d_model, 64),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Dropout(dropout),
            nn.Linear(64, 1),
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        x = x.unsqueeze(-1)
        x = self.input_proj(x)
        x = self.transformer(x)
        x = x[:, -1, :]
        return self.output_proj(x).squeeze(-1)


def train_returns_predictor(prices, lookback=60, horizon=5,
                            epochs=50, batch_size=32, lr=1e-4):
    """
    Fine-tune a transformer to predict future returns.
    """
    split = int(0.8 * len(prices))
    train_prices = prices[:split]
    val_prices = prices[split:]

    train_dataset = ReturnsDataset(train_prices, lookback, horizon)
    val_dataset = ReturnsDataset(val_prices, lookback, horizon)

    train_loader = DataLoader(train_dataset, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=True)
    val_loader = DataLoader(val_dataset, batch_size=batch_size)

    model = SimpleTSFM().to(DEVICE)
    optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr, weight_decay=0.01)
    scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=epochs)
    criterion = nn.MSELoss()

    best_val_loss = float('inf')
    best_state = None

    print(f"Training on {len(train_dataset)} samples...")
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        model.train()
        train_losses = []
        for features, targets in train_loader:
            features, targets = features.to(DEVICE), targets.to(DEVICE)

            optimizer.zero_grad()
            predictions = model(features)
            loss = criterion(predictions, targets)
            loss.backward()
            torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
            optimizer.step()
            train_losses.append(loss.item())

        model.eval()
        val_losses = []
        val_predictions, val_actuals = [], []
        with torch.no_grad():
            for features, targets in val_loader:
                features = features.to(DEVICE)
                predictions = model(features)
                val_losses.append(criterion(predictions.cpu(), targets).item())
                val_predictions.extend(predictions.cpu().numpy())
                val_actuals.extend(targets.numpy())

        avg_train = np.mean(train_losses)
        avg_val = np.mean(val_losses)

        pred_ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(val_predictions))
        actual_ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(val_actuals))
        rank_corr = np.corrcoef(pred_ranks, actual_ranks)[0, 1]

        if avg_val < best_val_loss:
            best_val_loss = avg_val
            best_state = model.state_dict().copy()

        scheduler.step()

        if (epoch + 1) % 10 == 0:
            print(f"Epoch {epoch+1}: Train MSE={avg_train:.6f}, "
                  f"Val MSE={avg_val:.6f}, RankIC={rank_corr:.4f}")

    model.load_state_dict(best_state)

    val_predictions = np.array(val_predictions)
    val_actuals = np.array(val_actuals)
    directional_accuracy = np.mean(
        np.sign(val_predictions) == np.sign(val_actuals)
    )

    print(f"\nFinal Results:")
    print(f"  Best Val MSE: {best_val_loss:.6f}")
    print(f"  Directional Accuracy: {directional_accuracy:.2%}")
    print(f"  RankIC: {rank_corr:.4f}")

    return model, best_val_loss, rank_corr

def ensemble_forecast(chronos_pipeline, kronos_model, kronos_tokenizer,
                      price_series, kline_history,
                      chronos_weight=0.4, kronos_weight=0.6):
    """
    Combine Chronos and Kronos predictions via weighted ensemble.
    The weights can be optimized via Bayesian optimization or
    online learning.
    """
    chronos_preds = chronos_forecast(
        chronos_pipeline, price_series, prediction_length=5, num_samples=50
    )
    chronos_point = np.median(chronos_preds[0], axis=0)
    chronos_returns = np.diff(np.log(chronos_point + 1e-8))

    kronos_returns = kronos_predict_returns(
        kronos_model, kronos_tokenizer, kline_history, prediction_horizon=5
    )

    ensemble_returns = (chronos_weight * chronos_returns +
                        kronos_weight * kronos_returns)

    return {
        'chronos': chronos_returns,
        'kronos': kronos_returns,
        'ensemble': ensemble_returns,
        'signal': np.sum(ensemble_returns),
    }

The Hard Truth: What Will Go Wrong

Foundation models for time series are not a magic bullet. Here is what the literature and practitioner experience reveal about their limitations.
1. The Financial Specificity Gap.
The most important finding in this space is that general time series foundation models fail on financial data. TimesFM's R² = −2.80 on financial tasks is not an anomaly - it is a structural problem. Financial time series violate the assumptions that make foundation models work:
Low signal-to-noise ratio. In NLP, there is rich semantic structure in text. In weather forecasting, physical laws constrain the dynamics. In financial markets, the signal-to-noise ratio is orders of magnitude lower. The autocorrelation of daily returns is typically between -0.05 and 0.05 - barely distinguishable from zero. A model pretrained on cleaner signals will overfit to noise in financial data.
Non-stationarity. Foundation models assume that temporal patterns are transferable across series. But financial markets are non-stationary by design: the data-generating process shifts as market participants adapt, new instruments are introduced, and macroeconomic conditions evolve. A pattern learned from 2020-2022 (zero-rate, high-momentum regime) may actively hurt in 2023-2024 (rising-rate, mean-reversion regime).
Adversarial dynamics. Markets are competitive. If a signal is discovered and exploited, it is arbitraged away. This creates a fundamental difference between financial forecasting and other domains: the act of prediction can invalidate the prediction. No weather model changes the weather by forecasting it.
2. The Black Box Problem.
Transformer-based models are opaque. When a trading signal is generated, you cannot ask the model why it predicted a positive return. This creates three problems:
  • Risk management: Without interpretability, it is impossible to bound the model's behavior under stress scenarios.
  • Regulatory compliance: Regulators increasingly require explainability for algorithmic trading systems.
  • Signal decay: When a signal stops working, you cannot diagnose whether the issue is data quality, regime change, or model degradation without interpretability tools.
Emerging techniques - attention visualization, SHAP values, gradient-based attribution - provide partial solutions, but transformer interpretability remains an active research area.
3. Tokenization Artifacts.
Each tokenization strategy introduces approximation error:
Quantization error (Chronos): Mapping continuous values to discrete bins loses information. The bin width Δ sets a precision floor. For high-frequency data where small price differences matter, this can be significant.
Patch boundaries (MOIRAI): Patch-level processing assumes that temporal structure within a patch is less important than cross-patch structure. Sharp discontinuities at patch boundaries can be missed.
BSQ approximation (Kronos): Spherical k-means clustering may not perfectly capture the true distribution of K-line patterns, especially for rare events (flash crashes, circuit breakers).
4. Inference Cost at Scale.
While 0.6ms inference (TimeGPT) is fast for a single series, a trading desk monitoring 5,000 equities with multiple features each needs careful engineering. The 710M-parameter Chronos Large model requires substantial GPU memory. Running hundreds of concurrent forecasts creates a compute bottleneck that can only be solved with model compression (quantization, pruning, distillation) or the MoE approach.
5. Heuristic Cross-Frequency Learning.
MOIRAI's authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation: the model has no explicit mechanism to relate patterns at different time scales. For financial applications, this is critical. A daily trend may be composed of intraday momentum and overnight gaps. A monthly pattern may emerge from the composition of weekly and daily dynamics. Without explicit multi-scale modeling, the model must learn these compositions implicitly - which may fail for complex financial dynamics.
6. Data Leakage Risk.
Foundation models are pretrained on massive corpora that may include the test data. Even with careful curation, the diversity of the pretraining corpus makes it impossible to guarantee that no information from the evaluation period leaked into training. This is particularly concerning for financial benchmarks, where the number of publicly available datasets is limited relative to the pretraining scale.
