The reader was not the problem.
You wrote it backwards.
Every email, every Slack message, every doc, every report you have ever sent. You wrote it the way a story is told. Setup first. Payoff last. You made the reader dig for the point. And the reader, who has 47 unread messages waiting, gave up before you got there.
There is a fix. It has existed since 1985. It costs nothing. And it is why McKinsey consultants get paid $500 an hour to write documents that a founder cannot put down.
It is called the Pyramid Principle. And once you turn it into 4 Claude prompts, every message you send for the rest of your life becomes impossible to ignore.

The Woman Who Cracked It

In 1963 a woman named Barbara Minto walked into Harvard Business School as one of only 8 women in a class of 600. When she graduated, McKinsey hired her as the first female MBA in the firm's history. They put her in the Cleveland office. Her job was to edit reports. (Wikipedia biography of Barbara Minto)
She noticed something everyone else had missed.
Every McKinsey consultant, no matter how brilliant, wrote reports the same way. They started with the background. They walked through the analysis. They built to the recommendation on the last page. And the partners, who were reading 20 of these a week, kept complaining about the same thing. They could not find the answer.
Minto started reorganizing every report she edited into a pyramid. The recommendation at the top. The supporting arguments in the middle. The data at the bottom. The Cleveland office manager, John Tomb, watched her method work so consistently that he made her train every associate in the firm. (Supernormal, Inside McKinsey's playbook)
By 1973 the Pyramid Principle was mandatory training at every McKinsey office in the world. She left the firm to teach it full time. She wrote the book in 1985. It has never gone out of print. (BetterUp explanation)
Today, 63 years later, every new McKinsey consultant still spends their first weeks learning her method. Amazon rebuilt its meeting culture around a version of it. Jeff Bezos banned PowerPoint at Amazon and required 6 page memos that follow her structure. (Management Consulted on the Amazon memo) Google analysts teach it. BCG and Bain teach it. Every consulting firm you have heard of teaches it.
And almost nobody outside those firms has ever heard of it.
That is the market gap. That is why you are still writing emails that get ignored while a McKinsey associate 2 years out of college writes a slide deck that a CEO reads in 90 seconds and says yes to.
The gap is not talent. The gap is a method she wrote down 40 years ago.

What You Are Doing Right Now

Here is what most people write when they need to make a point.
Hi team. So I have been thinking about our Q3 numbers. As you know, we have been running the same email cadence since March. I looked at the open rates. I looked at the click rates. I talked to the sales team. I ran the numbers three different ways. And I noticed a pattern. Weekday sends are underperforming. Weekend sends are outperforming. I think we should switch our schedule.
Read it again. Now count how many words the reader had to process before they got to the point.
87 words. The point was in the last 6.
That is the shape of a story. Setup. Middle. Payoff. It works when someone is watching a movie. It kills you when someone is scanning their inbox at a red light.
Minto's method flips this. Same message. Rewritten the McKinsey way.
We should move our email sends from weekdays to weekends. Three reasons. Weekday open rates dropped 34 percent since March. Weekend rates rose 22 percent over the same period. The sales team confirmed weekend replies convert 2 times faster. Data attached.
53 words. The point is in the first 7. Every following word answers a question the reader now cares about because they know where the story is going.
This is not a stylistic choice. This is a completely different technology for moving ideas from one brain to another. And you have been using the wrong one your entire career.

Phase 1: The Pyramid

Before you touch Claude, you need to see the shape.
A pyramid has three levels. Just three. Not five. Not seven.
Top: Your one answer. The recommendation. The bottom line. Written as a single sentence someone could tattoo on their arm.
Middle: 2 to 4 supporting points. Each one is a reason the top is true. They must be MECE. That word matters. Barbara invented it. MECE stands for Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. Your supporting points cannot overlap with each other. And together, they must cover every angle. No gaps. No repeats. (BetterUp on MECE)
Bottom: The data. The proof. The numbers, quotes, screenshots, and evidence that back up each middle point.
That is the whole shape. Three levels. Two rules. No exceptions.
The magic is in what this shape does to a reader's brain. Minto argued that humans naturally impose hierarchy on any information they receive. If you do not give them a hierarchy, they will invent one badly. If you give them yours, they trust you before they finish the first sentence.
The pyramid is not a template. It is a promise. Every word you write is either the answer, a reason for the answer, or proof of a reason. Nothing else survives.

Phase 2: The 4 Prompts

Here is the exact system. Copy the prompts. Save them. Use them today.

Prompt 1: The Answer First Prompt

This is the one you will use most. Every email. Every message. Every doc. Every proposal.
You are trained in the Barbara Minto Pyramid Principle.
I need to communicate this idea. Rewrite it in pyramid form.
Rules:
1. Line 1 is the single sentence answer or recommendation.
2. Then list 2 to 4 supporting points. They must be MECE.
3. Under each point, list the proof or data.
4. Cut every word that is not the answer, a reason, or proof.
5. If I have not given you the answer clearly, ask me one question that surfaces it.
Here is my idea: [PASTE YOUR MESSY DRAFT]
What this does. You paste 400 words of tangled thinking. Claude gives you back a 90 word pyramid. The answer sits on line one. The reader gets the point before they blink. The supporting logic follows like stairs.
Use this on every email longer than 3 sentences. Use this on every doc before you send it. Use this on every Slack message where you need a real answer.
You will feel a small pain the first ten times you do this. The pain is the story instinct dying. Let it die. Nobody has ever complained that a message was too clear.

Prompt 2: The SCQA Opener Prompt

The Pyramid tells you how to structure the middle and the bottom. The opening is a separate craft. Minto invented a framework for it called SCQA. Situation. Complication. Question. Answer. (Slideworks, Pyramid Principle guide)
Every McKinsey deck opens this way. Every good proposal opens this way. Every persuasive doc you have ever admired opens this way. You just did not have a name for it.
You are trained in Barbara Minto's SCQA framework.
Write me the opening for this document using SCQA.
Rules:
1. Situation. One sentence the reader already agrees with.
2. Complication. One sentence about what changed or what broke.
3. Question. The implied question the complication creates.
4. Answer. My recommendation in one sentence.
Total length: 4 sentences maximum.
Here is my topic: [PASTE YOUR TOPIC]
Here is my recommendation: [PASTE YOUR RECOMMENDATION]
What this does. The reader nods at line one because you are stating something they know. They lean in at line two because you named a problem they feel. They form the question in their head at line three before you ask it. And when you deliver the answer at line four, they receive it like a gift instead of a pitch.
This is how proposals get funded. This is how promotions get approved. This is how bad news gets accepted. Four sentences. In that order.

Prompt 3: The MECE Audit Prompt

Once you have your pyramid, one thing kills it faster than anything else. Your supporting points overlap. Or they leave a hole.
If two of your points say the same thing in different words, a smart reader spots it in 4 seconds and stops trusting you. If your points miss an obvious angle, a smart reader spots the gap and asks the question you were hoping to avoid.
MECE fixes both. Mutually Exclusive. Collectively Exhaustive. Every McKinsey partner runs this check on every deck before it leaves the office.
You are trained in Barbara Minto's MECE principle.
Audit these supporting points for MECE.
Rules:
1. Are any two points saying the same thing? Flag overlaps.
2. Is there an obvious angle missing? Flag gaps.
3. Suggest a rewritten set of 2 to 4 supporting points that are fully MECE.
4. Explain each suggested change in one sentence.
Here are my current supporting points: [PASTE YOUR 3 TO 5 POINTS]
Here is the top-level recommendation they support: [PASTE YOUR RECOMMENDATION]
What this does. Claude becomes your McKinsey partner. It stress tests your logic before your reader does. You either walk out with a bulletproof pyramid or you find the hole in your thinking before it embarrasses you in the meeting.
Run this on every important doc. Every board memo. Every proposal above . Every strategy note. Five minutes here saves you a career mistake.

Prompt 4: The Executive Test Prompt

The final McKinsey secret. Every good consultant has a partner who reads their draft and grades it brutally. Most people do not. This prompt gives you that partner.
Before you send anything important, run it through Claude one more time. Not to polish it. To find out if a busy executive would actually act on it.
You are a busy CEO with 200 unread emails.
You have 3 minutes before your next meeting.
Read this communication and answer honestly:
1. After reading only the first 2 sentences, do you know
what I want you to do? Yes or no.
2. After reading the full document, name the weakest,
most redundant, or missing argument.
3. Would you act on this, ask for more information,
or ignore it? Be brutal.
4. If you would ignore it, rewrite the first 2 sentences
so a busy CEO would not.
Here is my communication: [PASTE YOUR FINAL DRAFT]
What this does. You get the judgment of your harshest reader before the real one sees it. If Claude says a CEO would act, you send. If Claude says a CEO would ignore, you get a rewrite of the exact 2 sentences that failed.
This is the difference between shipping something and shipping something that works. Use it before every email above $1K in value. Every board memo. Every proposal. Every ask that matters.

The 10 Minute Workflow

All 4 prompts in one loop. Ten minutes. Every important document.
Minutes 1 to 5. Prompt 1. The Answer First Prompt. Your messy thoughts become a clean pyramid.
Minutes 5 to 8. Prompt 2. The SCQA Opener Prompt. Your pyramid gets a narrative opening.
Minutes 8 to 10. Prompt 3. The MECE Audit Prompt. Your logic gets stress tested.
Final minute. Prompt 4. The Executive Test Prompt. A simulated CEO grades whether you would get the yes.
Total: 10 minutes to turn any communication into something that reads like it came from a $500 an hour consultant. Not because the words are fancier. Because the structure is clearer.
Think bottom up. Communicate top down. That is the whole system in seven words.

Phase 3: The One Rule That Runs the Whole System

Every McKinsey consultant learns this rule in their first week. Once you learn it, you cannot unlearn it.
Lead with the answer.
Not the setup. Not the context. Not the background. Not the caveat. Not the polite throat clearing. The answer.
Your reader has a question. Whether they said it or not. Every message you send should answer that question in the first line. Then, and only then, do you get to explain why.
This is called BLUF in the military. Bottom Line Up Front. Same idea. Different acronym. (BetterUp explanation)
Every other habit in the McKinsey playbook flows from this one. One idea per slide. One recommendation per memo. One ask per email. Ruthless subtraction of anything that is not the answer, a reason, or proof.
The Pyramid Principle is not a writing tip. It is a rewiring of who you are as a communicator.
Once you internalize it, you cannot write the old way even if you try. Every time you start with background, a voice in your head will ask, where is the answer. Every time you bury the ask on line 6, that voice will move it to line 1. Every time you list 5 supporting points that repeat each other, that voice will demand MECE.
That voice is worth $500 an hour.

Phase 4: A Real Example

Here is a real transformation. Names changed. Everything else is what a real professional sent, before and after.
Before, the way most people write.
Hey Jamie, hope you had a great weekend. I wanted to circle back on the vendor conversation from last month. As we discussed, we have been evaluating three options for the new CRM. I have been going deep on each one over the past two weeks, and I wanted to share what I found. So, Vendor A is the cheapest but the integrations are limited. Vendor B has better features but the pricing is higher. Vendor C sits in the middle. I talked to references at each. I looked at the pricing carefully. I mapped out our must-haves. Based on all of that, I think we should probably go with Vendor B. But I wanted to run it by you first. Let me know what you think.
After, the McKinsey way.
Recommend Vendor B for our new CRM. Three reasons.
One. Vendor B is the only option with native Salesforce and Slack integrations, which our sales team uses daily.
Two. Total cost over 3 years is $4,200 higher than Vendor A but saves an estimated 12 hours per week across the team.
Three. All 4 reference customers at similar scale to us renewed after year 1.
Decision needed by Friday to lock the launch date. Full analysis attached.
Same information. Same recommendation. One reads like a memo. One reads like a whisper. Guess which one gets approved.

Why This Framework Has Survived 60 Years

The reason this method still works in 2026 is not magic. It is 3 things psychologists have measured for decades.
Cognitive load. The brain processes top down information faster than bottom up information. When you lead with the answer, the reader builds a mental frame that organizes everything after. When you bury the answer, the brain holds unorganized information in memory until the point arrives. That is strain. Strain makes people stop reading.
The primacy effect. People remember the first thing they read more than anything else. If the first thing is your conclusion, they remember your conclusion. If the first thing is your greeting, they remember your greeting. The Pyramid Principle weaponizes this by making sentence 1 the most important sentence.
Decision fatigue. Executives make hundreds of choices a day. Every email that needs decoding uses decision energy. A pyramided message needs zero decoding. The answer is there. The decision is made. The next email opens. That is why pyramid messages get faster replies and higher action rates.
Barbara Minto did not invent these effects. She just built the first communication system that respects them.

The 60 Second Test

Here is the fastest way to know if you are already in trouble.
Go to your Sent folder. Open the last 5 emails you wrote that were longer than 3 sentences. Read only the first sentence of each.
If those first sentences tell someone what you want, what you decided, or what needs to happen next, you are already halfway there.
If those first sentences say things like, "Hope your week is going well" or "I wanted to circle back" or "Just following up on our last conversation", you have been writing backwards. All of them. For years.
The good news is you can fix every future email starting with the next one. The 4 prompts are above. The rule is one line. Lead with the answer.
The people who will run the room in the next 10 years are not the smartest people in it. They are the ones whose messages nobody skips.
