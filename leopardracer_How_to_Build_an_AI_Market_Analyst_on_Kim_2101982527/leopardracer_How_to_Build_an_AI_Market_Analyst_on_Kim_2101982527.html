How to Build an AI Market Analyst on Kimi K3 and GPT-6 Astra: The Full Architecture
17万
random Tuesday, 9:47 AM NVDA jumps 6.8% in the first forty minutes after the open. Volume is running at 2.9x normal. Twitter is on fire: someone says Morgan Stanley upgraded the stock, someone says there's a chip leak, someone's convinced it's a short squeeze because they saw a screenshot of open interest. You open five tabs - Yahoo Finance, SEC EDGAR, an earnings transcript, a Bloomberg-adjacent account, a Reddit thread.
Forty minutes later you have thirty scattered facts and no idea which five of them actually explain the move and which twenty-five just happened to coincide with it in time.
That's not an information problem. It's a synthesis problem. And it's exactly what became solvable in 2026 once three things existed together: a brokerage with open on-chain and API access to market data (Robinhood), a model with an honest million-token context for comparing documents (Kimi K3, Moonshot AI), and a model built for heavy multi-step reasoning (GPT-6 Astra, OpenAI). Separately, tools. Together, a pipeline that turns eight hours of research into fifteen minutes of reading a finished conclusion.
What follows is how to build it - with real formulas (written so they survive being pasted anywhere), real worked numbers, and an explicit line between "this is how the math works" and "this is proof it works."

Why "prediction" is the wrong target

If your system outputs BUY/SELL/HOLD, you're on the hook for a forecast that, on average, doesn't beat the market on multi-month horizons - this is the well-documented finding of S&P's recurring SPIVA reports, which consistently show most active funds underperforming their benchmark over longer periods. No LLM fixes this, because the problem isn't analysis quality - it's market efficiency: an easily computed signal gets priced in by someone faster than you before you finish reading about it.
But there's a question an LLM answers well: what changed, and what's the actual basis for thinking it's real rather than noise?
That's compression of the present, not prediction of the future. It's testable and doesn't drown in randomness. Build on that.

Layer 1: data first, LLM second

The first mistake is feeding raw headlines straight into an LLM. An LLM is a reasoning engine, not a database - using it to extract basic facts from raw text is expensive and unreliable compared to just... having a database.
Collect, before any AI touches anything:
  • price and volume (minute + daily)
  • market cap
  • earnings dates and results, analyst estimate revisions
  • institutional holdings (13F - lagged up to 45 days, this matters a lot below)
  • insider transactions (Form 4)
  • options activity (put/call ratio, unusual activity)
  • news flow
  • broker customer-flow data where available
  • technical indicators

The database stores facts. The signal engine does arithmetic. The LLM only enters once the numbers say "something unusual is happening here."

Layer 2: turning data into signals

Volume anomaly
Volume ratio against the 20-day moving average:
volume_ratio = today's_volume / average_volume_20d
Worked example: average volume 11.8M, today's volume 34M →
volume_ratio = 34 / 11.8 ≈ 2.88
Almost 3x normal volume. Not "buy" - "go find out why."
Price momentum, normalized
5-day return:
return_5d = (price_today - price_5d_ago) / price_5d_ago
Worked example: $142 → $158 over 5 days:
return_5d = (158 - 142) / 142 = 0.1127 ≈ 11.3%
Is 11.3% a lot? Depends entirely on the stock. Normalize with a Z-score using that stock's own historical average and standard deviation of 5-day returns:
z_return = (return_5d - historical_mean) / historical_stdev
Worked example: mean 0.8%, stdev 3.4% →
z_return = (0.1127 - 0.008) / 0.034 ≈ 3.08
A Z-score above 3 is statistically rare under a normal distribution (roughly 0.1% probability) - though equity returns have fatter tails than normal, so treat "rare" as "worth a second look," not "impossible by chance." Do the same for volume to get zvolume​, then combine:
anomaly_score = |z_return| + |z_volume|
Higher score = more unusual event. Trigger for investigation, not a conclusion.

Institutional flow - and its main trap

13F filings lag up to 45 days after quarter-close. When you see a fund "just" increased a position, that data can already be six weeks stale. Always show the filing date next to the signal, not just the number.
position_change_pct = (shares_now - shares_before) / shares_before × 100
Worked example: 2,400,000 → 3,100,000 shares:
position_change_pct = (3,100,000 - 2,400,000) / 2,400,000 × 100 ≈ 29.2%
One fund is noise. Aggregate across funds, weighting each by its historical signal accuracy (some funds' filings reliably precede moves; some reliably lag them - this is something you check, not assume):
institutional_flow = Σ (weight_j × position_change_j)  for each fund j
The composite signal - and why the weights can't be made up
Most articles on this topic write "we use weights 0.15, 0.30, 0.25..." with zero explanation of where those numbers came from. That's not a signal, that's numerology.
The honest way: take historical events where anomaly_score > 2, label each "price rose 5%+ over the next 20 trading days" (yes/no), and fit a logistic regression:
P(rise) = 1 / (1 + e^-(b0 + b1·retail + b2·institutional + b3·fundamental + b4·options + b5·momentum))
The coefficients b1...b5 are your real weights - derived from data, and the sign can surprise you. Retail inflow that shows up after institutional inflow (the crowd chasing) often carries negative predictive weight.

Sequence matters more than the signals

Institutional buying → price rise → retail inflow is smart money first. Price rise → retail inflow → institutional filing showing up after is institutions chasing (or just the 45-day lag catching up).
lag_institutional_to_price = time_of_price_move - time_of_institutional_signal
lag_retail_to_price = time_of_price_move - time_of_retail_signal
A positive, large lag_institutional_to_price (signal came well before the move) reads one way. A negative one (signal only appears after the move, likely just filing lag) reads completely differently - and given the 45-day 13F delay, this happens constantly and is easy to misread if you don't track it explicitly.

Layer 3: Kimi K3 - for comparison, not just reading

Kimi K3 (Moonshot AI, released July 16, 2026): 2.8T parameters (MoE, 16 of 896 experts active per token), 1,048,576-token context, native image/video understanding. Pricing: $3/M input tokens, $15/M output tokens. Independent benchmarks place it in the top tier of Artificial Analysis's index, roughly on par with Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5.
A million tokens ≈ 750,000 words - eight quarterly reports, an annual report, investor decks, and news, loaded simultaneously.
The task that actually uses this: not "summarize this call" (any model does that) but "compare all eight of the last quarters and find every place management's language stayed the same while the underlying numbers moved." That's a comparison task, and long context is what makes it tractable in one pass instead of eight separate summaries you then have to reconcile by hand.
A consistency check between words and numbers
consistency = 1 - |economic_reality - management_claim| / max(|economic_reality|, |management_claim|)
Illustrative example: management says "accelerating growth" for three straight quarters (claim strength, normalized: 0.8), while revenue growth actually slows 22% → 14% → 9% (reality, normalized: 0.4):
consistency = 1 - |0.4 - 0.8| / max(0.4, 0.8) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5
0.5 is middling, not high. That's a concrete, checkable trigger - "rhetoric is diverging from the trend, dig further" - instead of a vague gut feeling.

Layer 4: GPT-6 Astra - heavy reasoning and the counter-thesis

GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI, released September 3, 2026): 1.05M-token context input, up to 128K output, five reasoning-effort levels, built-in computer use / shell / code interpreter / web and file search. API pricing: $10/M input, $50/M output ($1/M for cached-input reads - a 90% discount on repeated context). OpenAI's own benchmarks show it leading on ARC-AGI-3 and completing computer-use tasks roughly 47% faster than the prior generation.
This is for "what am I still missing?" - not document reading.

The contradiction engine

Ask "why is this company attractive?" and you get ten reasons it's attractive. Congratulations, you built a confirmation-bias machine.
Instead, run two independent passes:
Agent A: "Build the strongest bullish thesis for [company] using only the provided data. Cite a source for every claim."
Agent B: "Build the strongest bearish thesis for [company] using only the provided data. Cite a source for every claim."
Agent C (arbiter): "For every major claim in both theses: identify the source, check whether the data supports it, find contradicting evidence, flag unsupported assumptions, state what new information would change the conclusion."
thesis_gap = bull_strength - bear_strength
A large gap doesn't mean "buy" or "sell" - it flags exactly where the market can't agree with itself, which is exactly where more digging pays off. Agreement between the two agents is often the more reliable (if less exciting) signal.
A worked example: hypothetical NVDA move
This entire section is a constructed illustration, not a real event. The system flags:
return_5d = +11.2%   volume_ratio = 2.7   z_return = 3.1
institutional_flow = +1.4   fundamental_score = +1.8
A bad system says: "Bullish. Buy."
A good system runs a checklist:
  1. Was there a catalyst (earnings, guidance)?
  2. Did analysts revise estimates before or after the price moved?
  3. Did institutional buying precede the move, or is it only visible now because of the 45-day lag?
  4. Is retail chasing or leading?
  5. Does options activity confirm direction, or is it speculative noise?
  6. Did valuation expand faster than earnings expectations changed?
  7. Are competitors showing the same demand pattern - industry shift or one-company story?
An answer like "revenue grew 31%, but forward P/E expanded from 28x to 41x over the same window - the market is pricing in future growth faster than the company has actually delivered it" is a specific, checkable observation. "Bullish momentum confirmed" is confident-sounding noise.

Portfolio math

Contribution to portfolio return
portfolio_return = Σ (weight_i × return_i)
Worked example: 40% NVDA / 25% MSFT / 20% AAPL / 15% ETH, NVDA up 10% this week:
NVDA_contribution = 0.40 × 0.10 = 0.04 = +4pp to the portfolio
If the whole portfolio was up 4.3%, you now know almost all of it came from one position.
Concentration risk (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index)
concentration = Σ (weight_i²)
20 equal-weight assets:
concentration = 20 × (0.05)² = 0.05
One position at 50%, the rest spread across 19 assets:
concentration = 0.5² + 19 × (0.5/19)² = 0.25 + 0.013 = 0.263
Fivefold difference, same ticker count. Worth flagging automatically because nobody checks this by hand every day.

correlation(X,Y) = covariance(X,Y) / (stdev_X × stdev_Y)

Pipeline economics: why you can't just "ask Astra everything"

One deep Astra pass - 500,000 input tokens, 20,000 output tokens:
cost = 0.5M × $10 + 0.02M × $50 = $5 + $1 = $6 per company
Run across 10,000 companies daily:
10,000 × $6 = $60,000/day ≈ $1.3M/month
Not an architecture - a way to go bankrupt in a quarter. Hence the filtering funnel:
10,000 companies
  → deterministic rules → 800
  → lightweight classifier → 150
  → Kimi K3 deep read → 40
  → GPT-6 Astra reasoning → 8
  → human review → 2-3
Kimi K3 on 150 candidates (300K in / 8K out tokens each):
150 × (0.3M × $3 + 0.008M × $15) = 150 × ($0.9 + $0.12) ≈ $153
Astra on the final 40:
40 × $6 = $240
Total daily AI spend: ~$400-450, not $60,000. Optimize the funnel before you optimize the model.

The part almost nobody writes: regulation

If you're in the US and charging subscribers for output that analyzes specific tickers, you risk falling under "investment adviser" territory under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 - especially if outputs read as recommendations rather than neutral fact statements. The line: a general information service (no registration) vs. personalized investment advice (requires RIA registration, disclosure obligations, fiduciary duty).
Practical consequence: the system's language stays at "here's what changed, here's the source" and avoids "we recommend buying" or portfolio-specific advice. This is also why none of the sample reports below use a numeric score like "87/100" - a score presented as personalized guidance is a real regulatory exposure, not just a methodology weakness.
If the system connects to a brokerage account with trading capability, permissions need hard separation:
Market data       READ
Portfolio         READ
Research          READ
Orders            requires explicit human confirmation
Transfers         NO
Account settings  NO
Any brokerage offering agent-connected trading typically states the user remains responsible for actions taken by a connected agent - that doesn't remove the need to build in risk checks and explicit confirmation before anything that actually executes.

Backtesting: checking whether any of this means anything

This entire section is a worked example of the method, not a real study. Take historical events with anomaly_score > 2, compute forward returns:
forward_return_k = (price_at_t+k - price_at_t) / price_at_t   for k = 1, 5, 20, 60 days
Split into Group A (score > 2) and Group B (score < 0), compare means. Illustrative result: Group A averages +3.8% over 20 days, Group B averages +0.9%. There's a gap - but it guarantees nothing for a single trade, because a huge spread hides behind any average. Always check the distribution (median, stdev, share of losers), not just the mean, since one giant outlier can wreck a naive comparison.
precision = true_positives / (true_positives + false_positives)
recall = true_positives / (true_positives + false_negatives)
If 200 signals flagged "strong" and 74 led to a sustained move, precision is 37%. That sounds modest, but the base rate for a random 20-day window showing a 5%+ gain across the broad market usually runs 15-20%, so 37% is a real edge over chance - while still being far from a reliable forecast.

What you're actually selling

A professional analyst spends 6-8 hours on first-pass research per company. A well-built system cuts that to 20-30 minutes, because most of that time is spent extracting and cross-referencing facts scattered across sources - exactly what a million-token context does cheaply compared to human hours.
You're selling research velocity, not prediction. Predictions, on average, don't beat the market, and any honest product says so on page one. The fraud starts exactly where "explains the past" gets sold as "predicts the future." Don't let that confusion into your own product.
