A prediction market price is not a price.
It's a probability the crowd agreed on a moment ago.
And the second real news arrives, that number is wrong. Not forever. Just for the few minutes it takes everyone else to finish the same math you could have done in seconds. Especially when it comes to low liquidity markets...
That math has a name. It was written down in 1763, by a dead man, and it is the single most important tool for anyone who trades a number that is supposed to represent a chance.
Most traders think their job is to predict what happens.
It is not.
Your job is to figure out what the price should be after the new information, and get there before the crowd does.
Prediction is guessing the future. This is measuring a probability, watching evidence hit it, and updating that probability correctly and fast.
There is one formula for updating a probability correctly. There has only ever been one. This article is that formula, where it came from, the fast version desks actually use and exactly how to turn it into a trade on a prediction market.

The price is already a probability

Start with the thing most people never say out loud.
When a market says an outcome trades at 38 cents, it is telling you the crowd's probability for that outcome is about 38 percent. The price and the probability are the same object.
A contract at 0.38 is a crowd standing on "roughly a 38 percent chance." That is not a metaphor. On a prediction market it is literally what the number means.

This is the part retail gets wrong on instinct. News drops, and the untrained reaction is a feeling. "This is big, it should go way up." How much is way up? From 38 to what? 45? 60? 80? The feeling has no number in it, so the trade has no edge in it. You are guessing at the size of a move that the math can actually give you.
Retail asks: is this news good or bad?
A quant asks: given this news, what is the new probability, exactly, and how far is it from the price still sitting on my screen?
The gap between those two numbers is the entire trade. And there is one formula that produces the new number.

The 1763 formula

In 1763, the Royal Society published a paper titled An Essay Towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances.
The author, Reverend Thomas Bayes, had been dead for two years. His friend Richard Price found the essay in Bayes's papers, cleaned it up, and submitted it.
Ten years later the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace rediscovered the same idea independently and turned it into the foundation of modern statistical inference.
What they found is the only correct rule for updating a belief when evidence arrives.
P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) / P(E)
Four pieces, and each one is plain once you name it.
P(H) is your prior. The probability of the outcome H before the news. On a market, this is handed to you for free, it is the current price.
P(E | H) is the likelihood. If the outcome really were going to happen, how likely was it that you would see this particular piece of news? This is the one you have to think about.
P(E) is how likely the news was in general, across both worlds, the one where H happens and the one where it does not.
P(H | E) is the posterior. The updated probability after the news. This is the number you are trying to find, the price the market should move to.
In words: your new belief is your old belief, tilted by how much more the evidence fits the world where the outcome happens than the world where it does not.
Here is the intuition that makes it click.
Evidence is only worth something if it would happen more in one world than the other. A headline that is equally likely whether or not the event occurs tells you nothing, and the formula correctly leaves your probability unchanged.
A headline that would almost only appear if the event is coming moves you hard. Strong evidence is not loud evidence. It is evidence that separates the two worlds.
That is the whole engine. Everyone can see the news. The edge is turning it into the right number instead of a feeling.

Fast version desks actually use

The formula above is correct but clumsy to run in your head, because of that P(E) in the denominator. So nobody who does this for a living uses that form. They use odds.
Rewrite Bayes in odds and the ugly denominator cancels out:
posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio
The likelihood ratio is just how much more likely the news is if the outcome happens versus if it does not: P(E | H) divided by P(E | not H). Statisticians call it the Bayes factor. It is a single number that says how strong this piece of evidence is.
A ratio of 3 means the news is three times more consistent with the outcome happening. A ratio of 1 means it is noise. A ratio below 1 means it actually cuts against the outcome.
Now the update is one multiplication.
Take a market at 30 cents. Prior odds are 30 to 70, about 0.43. A real piece of news arrives that is three times more likely in the world where the outcome happens, so a likelihood ratio of 3.
posterior odds = 0.43 × 3 = 1.29
Turn that back into a probability: 1.29 / (1 + 1.29) = 0.56.
The market is at 30. The math says 56. That is a 26-point gap, and it appeared the instant the news did. You do not need the price to prove you right. You need to be holding before the crowd finishes the same calculation and drags the price up to meet your number.
And there is one more form, the one that explains why this is fast enough to trade on. Take the logarithm of the odds and the multiplication becomes addition:
log-odds(posterior) = log-odds(prior) + log(likelihood ratio)
In log-odds, evidence just adds up. Each new piece of independent news is a number you add to a running total. This is not a trick I invented. In World War II, Alan Turing and I.J.
Good used exactly this at Bletchley Park to break Enigma, adding up units of evidence, which Good called bans and decibans, to update the odds of a code setting faster than any other method of the era. The same accumulation logic runs a modern trading model that ingests headline after headline and keeps a live posterior.
The mechanism collapses to one line. You are not predicting the event. You are computing where the price should sit after the news, and trading the gap before the crowd finishes the same math.

Step one: let the market price be your prior.
Do not invent a prior out of thin air.
The current price is the crowd's aggregated belief, and on liquid markets the crowd is hard to beat cold. Start from the price, then move it only with genuinely new evidence. If you find yourself throwing out the market's number and substituting your gut, you are not updating, you are gambling with extra steps.
Step two: price the evidence, not the emotion.
For a new piece of news, ask one question: how much more likely is this if the outcome happens than if it does not? That is your likelihood ratio. Be honest and be conservative, because most news is weaker evidence than it feels. A dramatic headline that would have appeared either way has a likelihood ratio near 1 and deserves to move nothing.
Step three: update, compare, and only trade a real gap.
Multiply prior odds by the likelihood ratio, convert back to a probability, and compare it to the price. If your posterior is 56 and the market is 30, that is a live edge. If your posterior is 33 and the market is 30, there is nothing there once fees are counted. The formula does not just find trades, it tells you when to sit still, which is most of the time.
Step four: respect that the edge decays as the crowd catches up.
Bayesian updating tells you where the price is going, not that it will get there slowly. The gap closes as other people process the same information. The whole edge lives in the window between the news landing and the crowd finishing the reprice. You move in that window or you do not move at all.
Now the ways this kills people, because a formula this powerful punishes misuse:
Do NOT double-count evidence.
If the news is already sitting in the price, its likelihood ratio for you is 1, not 3. The edge is only in information the crowd has not priced yet. Trading on a story everyone already read is buying a posterior that is already the price.
Correlated headlines are one piece of evidence, not many.
Five outlets reporting the same leak is one fact echoing five times.
Multiply five likelihood ratios together and you will talk yourself into a wildly overconfident posterior and size into a gap that does not exist.
Count the underlying facts, not the number of times you saw them.
Garbage likelihoods produce garbage posteriors, confidently. The math is exact, which means it faithfully amplifies whatever numbers you feed it. An overconfident likelihood ratio gives you an overconfident edge and a position too large for what you actually know.
Calibrate honestly or the formula's precision works against you.
