Part 1: Your Bottleneck Was Never Ideas

Ask a solo quant why they are not making money and they will tell you they need a better signal.
They don't. They need more attempts.
Here is the math nobody puts on a slide. Alpha discovery is a survival funnel. Out of a hundred plausible ideas, maybe thirty survive honest out-of-sample testing. Of those, maybe nine survive transaction costs. Of those, maybe three survive a regime change. One is worth real capital, and it decays.
That ratio is roughly fixed. It is a property of markets, not of your intelligence.
So the only variable you actually control is how many ideas enter the funnel per week.
A discretionary builder sitting at a keyboard runs three or four honest experiments a week. Not because they are lazy - because each one demands pulling data, cleaning it, writing the backtest, arguing with the result, and starting over. That is a full day of work for one number.
A fund does not solve this by hiring smarter people. It solves it by hiring enough people that the funnel never stops moving. Renaissance is not one genius. It is a machine that never sleeps.
You cannot hire that machine. You can build it.
A trading loop is not a robot that places your trades. That is the least interesting thing it does. A trading loop is a research factory where execution happens to be the last station on the line. Once you see it that way, every design decision changes.
The question stops being "how do I get the agent to place a good trade" and becomes "how do I run two hundred experiments a week without a single one of them lying to me."
That second question is an engineering problem. Engineering problems have answers.

Part 2: The Object That Moves Through The System

Most people design a loop as a set of components. Data module, signal module, execution module, risk module. Boxes with arrows.
That model breaks the moment the system runs unattended, because it tells you nothing about state. It cannot answer the only question that matters at 3 a.m.: what exactly is this signal allowed to do right now?
So flip it. Do not design the components. Design the thing that flows through them.
In a working system, a signal is an object with a life. It gets born, it earns permissions, it loses them, it dies. Six states, and it can only move between them through a gate:
CANDIDATE - generated by research. Allowed to consume compute. Allowed to touch nothing else.
VALIDATED - survived falsification by an independent process. Allowed into paper trading.
PAPER - trading with fake money against live prices for a fixed minimum window. Allowed to build a track record.
PILOT - trading real capital at a deliberately insulting size. One quarter of one percent. Enough to feel slippage, not enough to matter.
LIVE - full sizing under the portfolio's risk budget.
RETIRED - demoted, killed, or aged out. Kept forever in the record, never resurrected without a new validation cycle.
Everything else in the architecture exists for one reason: to move a signal forward through a gate, or to throw it backward when it stops deserving its permissions.
That framing gives you something the box diagram never does. Every component now has a testable job. The verifier's job is not "check the signal" - it is "decide whether CANDIDATE becomes VALIDATED." The risk monitor's job is not "watch positions" - it is "demote LIVE to RETIRED fast enough that it does not matter."
And promotion is never a jump. A signal that has never traded a dollar does not get real size because the backtest is pretty. It walks up the ladder or it does not walk at all.

class State(str, Enum):
    CANDIDATE = "candidate"
    VALIDATED = "validated"
    PAPER     = "paper"
    PILOT     = "pilot"
    LIVE      = "live"
    RETIRED   = "retired"

GATES = {
    (State.CANDIDATE, State.VALIDATED): falsification_gauntlet,
    (State.VALIDATED, State.PAPER):     always,
    (State.PAPER,     State.PILOT):     min_days(30) & live_sharpe(0.8) & tracking_error_ok,
    (State.PILOT,     State.LIVE):      min_fills(200) & slippage_within_model,
}

def advance(signal, target):
    gate = GATES.get((signal.state, target))
    if gate is None:
        raise IllegalTransition(signal.state, target)   # no skipping steps
    verdict = gate(signal)
    ledger.append(signal.id, signal.state, target, verdict)  # always recorded
    if verdict.passed:
        signal.state = target
    return verdict
Notice what that code does not have: a path from CANDIDATE straight to LIVE. Not because it would be hard to write. Because the day you are excited about a signal is exactly the day you would use it.

Part 3: The Five Ways An Unattended Loop Kills You

Every autonomous system I have built or torn apart failed in one of five ways. Not five categories of bugs - five specific structural failures. Each one has an architectural answer, and none of the answers is "prompt more carefully."
Failure 1: The agent says it is finished, and it is not
This is the quietest killer. An agent completes half a job, emits a completion signal in good faith, and the loop moves on. In coding you get a broken build. In trading you get a position that was supposed to be hedged, and is not.
The root cause is that the agent is both the worker and the judge of whether the work is done.
The fix is that no stop condition may be a claim. Every terminal state must be verifiable by something that did not do the work. Not "the agent reports the rebalance is complete" - instead: broker position count equals target position count, net exposure inside band, every intended order has a fill ID or a documented rejection. If the loop cannot prove it, the loop is not done and escalates.
Write your stop conditions as assertions against external state, never as questions you ask the model.
Failure 2: The maker grades its own homework
The agent that generated a signal is structurally the worst possible judge of it. It has already spent its reasoning justifying the idea. Ask it to check itself and it will confirm itself with impressive rigor.
This is not an AI quirk. It is why the trader who proposes a position at a serious shop is not the person who approves it, and why the researcher who builds a model does not sign off on it. The separation is the control.
But copying "maker and checker" as two agents is not enough. Most people build a checker that re-runs the same backtest and gets the same answer. Useless. The checker's job is not to reproduce the result. It is to falsify it.
That means the checker gets a different mandate, a different model, and data the maker never touched:
FALSIFICATION_GAUNTLET = [
    HoldoutTest(years=2, never_seen_by_maker=True),
    PurgedWalkForward(embargo_days=5),      # no leakage across the split
    CostModel(spread + impact + fees, stress_multiplier=2.0),
    ShuffledLabelControl(runs=200),          # must fail on scrambled data
    RegimeSplit(["2020Q1", "2022", "2024"]), # must survive all, not average
]
The fourth one is the test almost nobody runs and it catches the most. Scramble the labels and re-run the pipeline. If the "alpha" still shows up on noise, you have not found a signal — you have found a bug in your backtester. I have killed more strategies with that single control than with every Sharpe threshold combined.
The verdict is binary and it is the checker's alone. The maker gets a reason code, not a negotiation.
Failure 3: The loop reruns and doubles your position
This one costs real money the first time and it is entirely mechanical.
An unattended loop will retry. The network will time out after the order was accepted. The container will restart mid-cycle. The scheduler will fire twice on a clock change. If your execution path is not idempotent, every one of those events becomes a duplicate position, and you find out from your broker statement.
The fix: every order carries a deterministic key derived from intent, not from time.
key = sha256(f"{signal.id}|{symbol}|{target_qty}|{bar_timestamp}").hexdigest()[:32]

if store.seen(key):
    return store.result(key)        # replay, never re-send
broker.submit(order, client_order_id=key)   # broker dedupes too
store.commit(key, result)
Then, before every cycle, reconcile: pull actual positions from the broker and treat that as truth. Never trust your own memory of what you sent. The database is a cache; the broker is reality.
Failure 4: Amnesia - the loop repeats last month's disaster
An agent forgets between runs. That is fine and expected. The failure is when the system forgets, and the same drawdown happens twice for the same reason.
Most people solve this with a memory file full of prose. "We learned that earnings weeks are risky." That is a diary, not a control. The next run reads it, nods, and does the same thing.
Lessons must become constraints, not sentences. An incident is only closed when it exists as executable code with a test attached:
INC-0042  2026-03-08  -6.1%  sector concentration
  cause: five correlated names entered on the same factor
  constraint: max_sector_gross = 0.30
  enforced: risk/constraints.py::sector_cap
  test: tests/test_sector_cap.py::test_blocks_2026_03_08_basket
The test replays the exact conditions that produced the loss and asserts the system now refuses. Now the lesson cannot decay, cannot be politely ignored, and cannot be re-learned expensively.
Over a year this file stops being documentation and becomes the actual shape of your strategy - the accumulated scar tissue that makes the system narrower and better than the one you designed.
Failure 5: Silent drift - the world moved and nobody told the loop
The worst failure has no error, no exception, no alert. The strategy simply stops being true. Spreads widen, a competitor arrives, the correlation that carried the signal inverts. The loop keeps executing with total confidence into a market that no longer exists.
Drawdown limits catch this eventually, but "eventually" is the expensive part. You want to know before the P&L tells you.
So monitor the inputs, not just the outcome. The live distribution of your features against the distribution the strategy was validated on. Fill rates against the model. Realized slippage against predicted. Signal turnover against baseline. When live behavior separates from validation behavior beyond tolerance, demotion is automatic - LIVE drops to PILOT, PILOT drops to PAPER - before the loss shows up.
And behind all of it, a deadman switch. If the risk monitor stops writing a heartbeat for ninety seconds, flatten everything. The most dangerous state for an autonomous system is not "wrong." It is "trading with its safety layer dead and no idea."

Part 4: Build The Brakes Before The Engine

Everyone builds this in exactly the wrong order. Signal generation first, because it is the fun part. Risk last, because it is not.
The correct order is the reverse, and it is not close:
One. The ledger. Append-only record of every decision, transition, order and verdict. Build it first, because a system you cannot audit is a system you cannot debug, and an unattended loop you cannot debug is a slot machine.
Two. The kill switch. Flatten-everything, callable from anywhere, tested against a live broker in a sandbox. Plus the deadman switch. You need to be able to stop the machine before you let it start.
Three. Data ingestion with quality gates. A stale feed is worse than no feed, because it looks fine. Assert freshness, completeness, and sanity on every pull, and halt the pipeline rather than pass bad data downstream.
Four. The paper loop. The whole cycle, end to end, with zero real capital. Run it for a month. This is where you discover the ten mechanical bugs that would have cost money.
Five. Verification. The gauntlet, independent, adversarial.
Six. Signal research. The part you wanted to build on day one. It arrives last, and it arrives into a system that can survive it being wrong.
Seven. Capital, walked up the ladder. Pilot size, then real size, then a size that matters - each promotion earned by live evidence, never by a backtest.
Six of those seven steps are about containing failure. That ratio is not pessimism. It is the entire reason the system can be left alone.

Part 5: What It Looks Like At Ninety Days

The interesting property of this architecture is not that it runs while you sleep. It is what it becomes.
Month one, the loop is naive and generous. It generates dozens of candidates and the gauntlet murders almost all of them. The constraint file has four lines in it. You feel like you built an expensive machine for saying no.
Month three, the constraint file has thirty lines, and each one is a specific loss that can no longer repeat. The search space has narrowed. The candidates that survive are structurally different from what you would have written by hand, because they had to pass tests you would have rationalized your way around.
Month nine, the system knows things you do not consciously know — that this factor decays after the open, that this venue's fills lie during the first minute, that this whole family of ideas has never once survived the shuffled-label control. It is not smarter than you. It has simply never forgotten anything, and never made an exception because it was in a good mood.
That is the compounding. Not returns compounding, though hopefully that too. Constraints compounding. A search space that gets tighter and truer every month while your competitors are still re-learning the same lesson every quarter.

Summary

Alpha discovery is a survival funnel with a roughly fixed pass rate, so throughput is the only lever you fully control. A loop gives you throughput.
Design the signal's lifecycle, not the module diagram - six states, gates between them, promotion earned in steps and never skipped.
Then engineer against the five structural failures: stop conditions that are claims, makers who grade themselves, non-idempotent execution, lessons that stay prose, and drift that arrives without an error message. Every one has an architectural answer, and none of them is a better prompt.
Build the brakes first. The engine is the easy part, and it is worthless attached to a car that cannot stop.
So - the question worth sitting with.
You already know what your system does when everything works. Do you know, precisely, what it does at 3 a.m. when the feed goes stale, the fill never confirms, and nobody is watching?
If you cannot answer that in one sentence, that is the thing to build this week.
