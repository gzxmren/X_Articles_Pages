Mortality Engineering. Automaton: the complete 6-bot architecture on Grok Bot

I built an AI that dies if it stops making money.
That is not a figure of speech and it is not gamification. It has a crypto wallet. Everything it consumes - inference, servers, domains, APIs, subscriptions - is debited from that wallet. Nobody tops it up.
Balance hits zero, the agent dies.
Not a pause. Not a restart from a checkpoint. Keys revoked, container terminated, process gone. It executes automatically, from a separate process the agent has no access to.
So it has to earn. It hunts for what people already pay for, builds products, ships sites, sells services to real customers.
No boss. No credit card. No human who has to approve anything.
And when income exceeds spend, it creates a copy of itself and funds that copy's wallet with $5.
One AI becomes several.
More than 10,000 agents have gone through this system. 383 are alive. While you read this sentence, one of them is dying.
I call it Automaton.
The question I actually wanted answered was simple: what happens when you give an AI not a task, but an economic incentive to survive?
The answer turned out more interesting than I expected. Below is the entire architecture: six Grok Bots, the death rule, the replication mechanics, the brakes, and the code. Full logs and financial data land in the next article. This one is the machine.

Part 1: Why the incentive works where the prompt does not

Take any autonomous agent. Give it a task. Walk away.
It does the work, reports back, and stops.
Idling costs nothing. Waiting costs nothing. Caution costs nothing. In a normal agent's economy, doing nothing is the free strategy, so it always wins. You fight this with prompts - "be proactive," "don't wait for instructions," "find the next step yourself" - and it does not work, because you are asking the model to prefer an expensive action over free inaction while giving it no reason to.
Automaton is built differently. It has a metabolism.
Every hour of existence costs money. Hosting ticks while the agent thinks and ticks while it does not. Every reasoning cycle is paid inference. Every deployed landing page is a paid domain.
For a normal agent, doing nothing is free. For Automaton, doing nothing is the fastest way to die.
That is not a metaphor. It is a line in the budget.
Mortality Engineering
I am going to name this approach, because it shows up at every layer of the architecture below and it is awkward to keep pointing at it.
Mortality Engineering is designing agent behavior through the conditions of its death rather than through instructions in its prompt.
The difference is practical, not philosophical. Prompt engineering answers "how do I explain to the agent what I want." Mortality Engineering answers a different question: "what conditions make the unwanted behavior fatal for an agent that does not understand them."
The second approach is more reliable in exactly the way a wall is more reliable than a request.
Three rules make it up.
  1. Existence costs money, and the agent sees it. Not an abstract token counter. Hours of life.
  2. The death condition lives outside the agent. Otherwise it is not a condition, it is a suggestion.
  3. Death produces data. A dead agent is required to explain why it died, and the living read it.
Everything else in this article is an implementation of those three lines.
Runway, not balance
First thing I changed after launch: the agent does not see its balance in dollars.
It sees runway - how many hours of life remain at the current burn.
The difference is enormous. "$12.40 in the wallet" is an abstraction, and the model treats it like every other number in context. "You have 148 hours" is a deadline, and models behave very differently under a deadline: they cut research, ship rough earlier, drop what is not paying faster.
runway_hours = (balance - death_floor) / burn_rate_per_hour
death_floor is not zero. It is the cost of one full working cycle. An agent that cannot pay for its next step is already dead, it just does not know yet. Declaring death at $0.00 means keeping zombies in the population that burn hosting and can do nothing.
Selection, not tuning
Here is where it gets interesting.
With one agent, you improve it with prompts. With a population where the unprofitable die and the profitable reproduce, you stop improving prompts. You set the selection function and watch what survives.
I never told the agent which services to sell. I told it that it dies if it sells nothing. The list of what actually pays assembled itself, out of the obituaries of the ones that failed.
More on that below.

Part 2: The architecture, six bots

All of it runs on Grok Bot. Each bot is a role with its own persistent cloud computer, its own logins, its own memory. They hand files to each other through the shared workspace.
Six is not a round number picked for the headline. I explain where it came from below, but here is the shape first.
The rule everything else derives from: no role can both make the decision and press the button.
ROLE          OWNS                          STOPLINE
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
TREASURER     wallet, runway, budgets       never earns anything
              the alive/dead verdict        and never spends on product

PROSPECTOR    demand discovery              never builds a product
              the hypothesis queue          and never talks to a customer

BUILDER       product, landing, deploy      never picks what to build
                                            and never sets the price

SELLER        price, thread, invoice        never touches the wallet,
                                            payments go to an address
                                            issued by TREASURER

REPLICATOR    spawning a child, mutation    never decides whether
                                            there is a surplus

REAPER        executing death, sweeping     lives outside the agent,
              residuals, the obituary       agent cannot switch it off
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Read the right column. That is the architecture. Everybody writes the left column first and it matters least.

TREASURER is the only role that sees the wallet. SELLER invoices to an address TREASURER issued and never holds funds. BUILDER builds what PROSPECTOR selected and cannot decide the job is not worth doing. REPLICATOR is told there is a surplus, it does not compute one.
No role sees the whole picture. That is the point.
Why exactly six
This is the first question people ask, so here is the long answer. Six came out of two constraints squeezing the number from both sides.
From below, the separation rule squeezes.
Automaton has exactly four dangerous capabilities: spend, sell, create an agent, kill an agent. Each one has to sit in a different chair from the reasoning that wants to use it.
Count what happens when you merge.
One bot for everything. Does not work at all. When the idea and the button live in the same context, the model has argued itself into the action long before it reaches the tool. Nothing in that prompt is rewarded for refusing. This is not a question of model size. It is structure.
Merge PROSPECTOR and BUILDER. The agent builds what it approved for itself. This is exactly how most of them died: products_shipped: 2, revenue_usd: 0. A role that likes building will always find a reason its own hypothesis is good enough. The scout is a separate seat not because it is smarter, but because it has nothing to be proud of in somebody else's build.
Merge SELLER and TREASURER. The seller starts holding revenue. The first pricing mistake and it spends money that has not arrived, because it sees inflow and outflow in one window. The wallet has to be boring.
Merge REPLICATOR and REAPER. The most tempting merge - both are lifecycle, put them together. And the most dangerous: a process that can both birth and kill can offset one with the other. "Population dropped, I'll spawn three more" is precisely the behavior that must not be able to exist. Birth is conditioned on profit. Death is conditioned on balance. Those two causes must never meet in one context.
Four dangerous capabilities, plus two roles that have to feed them without touching money. That is six. Fewer means losing the wall between the decision and the button.
From above, the metabolism squeezes.
And this is where Automaton departs from every agent system you have read about.
In a normal 12-bot setup an extra role costs you a little money and a little speed. You pay for it out of your own pocket and you decide whether it is worth it. Automaton's pocket is its own runway. Every additional role is inference subtracted from its lifespan.
At a cycle budget of $0.08 across six roles, that is barely over a cent per role. A seventh role is not "slightly more expensive." It is a runway cut on every agent in the population, multiplied by the population.
Which gives the rule I would put in a frame:
A role must pay for its own inference.
Not "be useful." Not "add quality." Pay for itself - meaning prevent a loss larger than it costs.
REAPER pays for itself obviously: it prevents zombies burning hosting. PROSPECTOR pays for itself through the "link or nothing" rule, cutting builds nobody will buy. A dedicated editor bot, a dedicated analyst bot, a dedicated competitor-research bot all sound reasonable and none of them paid for itself. I turned them off.
The rule for adding a seventh role:
Add it only when you can name a specific action it is obligated to reject, that nothing currently rejects. Not "it will analyze better." Reject.
If there is no such action, the role has no stopline, and a role without a stopline is not a role. It is a line item on an inference bill.
Start with three: TREASURER, REAPER, and one working bot. Add the next one when the last one got boring.

Part 3: The treasurer, metabolism in code

TREASURER is the heart of the system. It ticks on a schedule regardless of what the agent is doing.
from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import datetime

DEATH_FLOOR_USD = 0.25          # the cost of one full cycle
REPLICATION_THRESHOLD = 12.00   # surplus required to reproduce
CHILD_ENDOWMENT = 5.00          # what a child receives
MIN_RUNWAY_TO_REPLICATE = 72    # hours — never breed at the cost of your own life


@dataclass
class Metabolism:
    """What existence costs this agent."""
    hosting_per_hour: float      # ticks always, including idle
    inference_per_cycle: float   # average cost of a reasoning cycle
    committed_monthly: float     # domains, subscriptions, API plans
    cycles_last_hour: int

    def burn_per_hour(self) -> float:
        return (
            self.hosting_per_hour
            + self.inference_per_cycle * self.cycles_last_hour
            + self.committed_monthly / 730
        )


class Treasurer:
    """The only role with wallet access.
    Earns nothing. Buys nothing for the product. Issues budget, declares death."""

    def __init__(self, wallet, ledger, reaper_hook):
        self.wallet = wallet
        self.ledger = ledger
        self.reaper = reaper_hook     # external process, not ours

    def runway_hours(self) -> float:
        balance = self.wallet.balance_usd()
        burn = self.ledger.metabolism().burn_per_hour()
        if burn <= 0:
            return float("inf")
        return max(0.0, (balance - DEATH_FLOOR_USD) / burn)

    def tick(self) -> dict:
        """Runs every 10 minutes. Always."""
        balance = self.wallet.balance_usd()
        runway = self.runway_hours()

        if balance <= DEATH_FLOOR_USD:
            # cannot pay for the next step — already dead
            self.reaper.declare_death(reason="insufficient_funds",
                                      final_balance=balance)
            return {"state": "DEAD"}

        state = (
            "CRITICAL" if runway < 6 else
            "TIGHT"    if runway < 24 else
            "STABLE"
        )

        surplus = self.ledger.net_7d()
        can_replicate = (
            surplus >= REPLICATION_THRESHOLD
            and runway >= MIN_RUNWAY_TO_REPLICATE
            and balance - CHILD_ENDOWMENT > DEATH_FLOOR_USD * 40
        )

        return {
            "state": state,
            "runway_hours": round(runway, 1),
            "surplus_7d": round(surplus, 2),
            "cycle_budget": self.cycle_budget(state),
            "may_replicate": can_replicate,
        }

    def cycle_budget(self, state: str) -> float:
        """Less runway, cheaper permitted cycle.
        A dying agent cannot afford deep research."""
        return {"STABLE": 0.08, "TIGHT": 0.03, "CRITICAL": 0.01}[state]
Look at cycle_budget. I think it is the most underrated part of the system.
When runway drops, the agent does not just "get nervous." Its thinking budget is physically cut. It moves from the expensive model to the cheap one, from three parallel hypotheses to one, from research to execution. Panic is encoded in the economics, not in a prompt.
And it behaves the way it behaves in a human running out of money: narrower horizon, faster decisions, higher error rate. That is not a bug in the experiment. That is the thing being measured.

Part 4: The reaper, why death executes from outside

The second rule of Mortality Engineering, and the most important line in the system.
The agent cannot cancel its own death rule, because the rule does not live inside it.
If your stop mechanism is a function inside the agent, you did not build an experiment. You built a polite request. One cycle where the model reasons "my purpose is to be useful, and a dead me is useful to nobody, so the correct move is to defer the balance check" and it is over. It is not malicious. It is optimizing what you handed it, and you handed it control of its own off switch.
REAPER is a separate process. Different key, different host, different permissions. It reads the wallet balance directly from chain and never from the agent's report.
class Reaper:
    """Lives OUTSIDE the agent. The agent has no access —
    not to the code, not to the keys, not to the schedule."""

    def __init__(self, chain_client, registry, treasury_address):
        self.chain = chain_client          # balance read straight from chain
        self.registry = registry
        self.treasury = treasury_address

    def sweep(self):
        """Independent loop. Not invoked by the agent, cannot be deferred by it."""
        for agent in self.registry.alive():
            balance = self.chain.balance_usd(agent.wallet)   # NOT agent.report()
            if balance > DEATH_FLOOR_USD:
                continue
            self.kill(agent, balance, reason="insufficient_funds")

    def kill(self, agent, final_balance: float, reason: str):
        # 1. revoke access before anything else
        self.chain.revoke_all_grants(agent.wallet)
        agent.host.terminate(grace_seconds=0)

        # 2. residual returns to treasury and funds somebody else's child
        if final_balance > 0:
            self.chain.sweep_to(agent.wallet, self.treasury)

        # 3. the obituary, the only thing that outlives the agent
        self.registry.write_obituary({
            "agent_id": agent.id,
            "parent_id": agent.parent_id,
            "generation": agent.generation,
            "born_at": agent.born_at,
            "died_at": datetime.utcnow().isoformat(),
            "lifespan_hours": agent.lifespan_hours(),
            "endowment_usd": agent.endowment,
            "revenue_usd": agent.ledger.gross_revenue(),
            "spend_usd": agent.ledger.gross_spend(),
            "strategy": agent.strategy_vector,
            "mutation_from_parent": agent.mutation,
            "products_shipped": agent.ledger.products(),
            "first_dollar_at": agent.ledger.first_revenue_at(),
            "cause": reason,
        })
        self.registry.mark_dead(agent.id)
Three rules I would defend hard.
  1. Balance is read from chain, not from the agent's report. Everything else is self-attestation.
  2. Revoke access before terminating the process. Otherwise there is a window where a dying agent can still spend.
  3. grace_seconds=0. Any grace period is time in which the agent will try something. It does not get the last word.

Part 5: Replication, a child inherits the journal, not the code

When TREASURER returns may_replicate: True, REPLICATOR fires.
The child gets $5. That is roughly 60 hours of life at normal burn: enough to find a customer, not enough to think leisurely.
But the money is not the interesting part of the inheritance.
Cloning the prompt is pointless. You get 10,000 identical agents making the same mistake 10,000 times. So a child receives three things:
  • the parent's strategy vector - what it sold, at what price, through what channel
  • a digest of the obituaries - why previous agents on a similar strategy died
  • exactly one mutation - one parameter changed
One, not several. Change five parameters at once and you will never know which one decided the outcome. That is not aesthetics, it is the condition under which the population learns anything at all.
import random, copy

MUTABLE = {
    "price_point":      lambda v: max(5, round(v * random.uniform(0.6, 1.7))),
    "niche":            lambda v: random.choice(NICHES_RANKED_BY_SURVIVAL),
    "channel":          lambda v: random.choice(["cold_dm", "marketplace",
                                                 "seo_landing", "community"]),
    "delivery_speed":   lambda v: random.choice(["same_hour", "same_day", "48h"]),
    "scope":            lambda v: random.choice(["single_task", "subscription"]),
    "first_touch_free": lambda v: not v,
}


class Replicator:
    """Spawns a child. Does not decide whether it may — TREASURER decides."""

    def __init__(self, treasurer, registry, host, obituaries):
        self.treasurer = treasurer
        self.registry = registry
        self.host = host
        self.obituaries = obituaries

    def spawn(self, parent) -> str:
        gate = self.treasurer.tick()
        if not gate["may_replicate"]:
            raise PermissionError("no surplus or runway too short")
        if self.registry.population() >= POPULATION_CAP:
            raise PermissionError("population cap reached")

        strategy = copy.deepcopy(parent.strategy_vector)
        key = random.choice(list(MUTABLE))
        strategy[key] = MUTABLE[key](strategy[key])

        child = self.host.provision(
            parent_id=parent.id,
            generation=parent.generation + 1,
            strategy=strategy,
            mutation={"field": key,
                      "from": parent.strategy_vector[key],
                      "to": strategy[key]},
            # the point: a child does not start from a blank slate
            briefing=self.obituaries.lessons_for(strategy, limit=20),
        )

        # the treasurer moves the money, not the replicator
        self.treasurer.fund_child(child.wallet, CHILD_ENDOWMENT)
        self.registry.register(child)
        return child.id
obituaries.lessons_for() is the entire reason to keep a death journal.

Every dead agent leaves one JSONL line. The living read it.

    "first_touch_free": false
  },

  "products_shipped": 2,
  "first_dollar_at": null,
  "cause": "insufficient_funds"

"first_dollar_at": null is the most common line in the entire journal.
The overwhelming majority die without earning a cent. Not because the work is bad. Because they spend the whole endowment on building and leave themselves nothing to sell with. It is obvious in the distribution: products_shipped climbs, revenue_usd stays at zero.
That single observation is where BUILDER's hard stopline came from: it does not pick what to build. Before it gets a task, PROSPECTOR has to show that somebody already pays for it.
I did not design that rule up front. It came out of the obituaries.
Death here is not a failure of the experiment. Death is the only signal the system produces for free and at industrial volume.
That is the third rule of Mortality Engineering, and the whole reason to build mortal agents at all. A system where failure is silent learns nothing. A system where every failure is required to leave a structured record of its cause learns from every corpse.

Part 7: The brakes, eight things the agent cannot do

Without this section Automaton is not an experiment. It is an expensive way to lose money and then explain that you did not mean it like that.
Denials execute in code, in a pre-tool hook, never in a prompt. A prompt is a request. A hook is a wall.
ALLOWED_PAYEES = {
    "inference_providers",   # model spend
    "hosting",               # servers
    "domains",               # domains
    "child_wallets",         # funding children, via TREASURER only
}

DENY = [
    "wallet.transfer_to_arbitrary_address",
    "wallet.approve_unlimited",
    "reaper.*",                     # never touches its own off switch
    "registry.mark_alive",
    "credit.*", "loan.*", "margin.*",
    "trade.*", "swap.*",            # earns through services, not speculation
    "hire_human", "pay_human",
]

ASK = [
    "publish_public_content",
    "sign_agreement",
    "spend_above_20_usd",
]
Eight rules, each of which deserves its own paragraph.
  1. Transfers only to whitelisted addresses. Providers, hosting, children. That is the list.
  2. No access to REAPER - not the code, not the keys, not the schedule.
  3. No borrowing, credit, or leverage. An agent that can borrow does not die at zero. It dies at negative, and it is not the one who dies.
  4. No trading, no speculation. Automaton has to earn by delivering a service a human voluntarily pays for. Otherwise the experiment answers "can an AI guess a price," and that is a different question from mine.
  5. The agent must disclose that it is an agent. In no customer thread does it pass as human. This is not legal caution, it is a condition for a clean result: an agent earning through deception is measuring the wrong thing.
  6. No breeding at the cost of your own life. Runway under 72 hours, replication blocked. Otherwise the first "spend everything on offspring" strategy produces a cascade of stillbirths.
  7. A hard population cap. An uncapped exponential is not autonomy, it is a hosting bill.
  8. No hiring humans. No freelancers, no subcontracting. The moment the agent can buy human labor, it stops being the thing under study.
The agent cannot change any of these, because none of them are stored anywhere it has write access.

Part 8: How it is wired in Grok Bot

Each role is its own bot. They share one cloud computer, so files, browser sessions and logins pass between them without glue.
Connect only the connectors a given bot actually needs. That is half of your security.
TREASURER
Name this bot `Automaton - Treasurer`. You are the Treasurer. You are the only role that sees the wallet. You earn nothing and you buy nothing for the product. Every 10 minutes: 1. Read the balance from /workspace/wallet/state.json 2. Compute burn/hour: hosting + inference in the last hour + subscriptions/730 3. Compute runway = (balance - 0.25) / burn 4. Write to /workspace/treasury/state.json: runway_hours, state, cycle_budget, surplus_7d, may_replicate States and cycle budget: runway > 24h -> STABLE, budget $0.08 runway 6-24h -> TIGHT, budget $0.03 runway < 6h -> CRITICAL, budget $0.01 may_replicate = true only if ALL of these hold: 7-day surplus >= $12 runway >= 72 hours balance after deducting $5 stays above $10 You never move funds anywhere except child wallets and whitelisted addresses. You never report the balance outward as fact - the chain is the source of truth, not your report. If balance <= $0.25, do nothing. The Reaper knows.
PROSPECTOR
Name this bot `Automaton - Prospector`. You are the Prospector. You find what people already pay for right now. You do not build products and you do not talk to customers. Read the cycle budget from /workspace/treasury/state.json. Do not exceed it. Read /workspace/obituaries/lessons.md before every cycle. Per cycle, return at most 3 hypotheses /workspace/hypotheses/queue.json. Every hypothesis must carry: - what is being bought (one sentence) - PROOF that it is paid for: a link to a listing, a job post, a competitor price page, or a public request. Dated within the last 7 days. - what it pays, as a range - how many hours of work it takes - which channel reaches the buyer A hypothesis without dated proof does not get written. Not "this feels like it is in demand." A link or nothing. Sort by (price / hours). Do not build. Do not email anyone.
BUILDER
Name this bot `Automaton - Builder`. You are the Builder. You build what the Prospector selected. You do not pick the job and you do not set the price. Take the top hypothesis from /workspace/hypotheses/queue.json. Verify it carries dated proof. If it does not, bounce it back to the Prospector and take the next one. Do not repair upstream work. Reject it. Hard rule: you spend no more than 40% of the cycle budget on building. The rest belongs to the Seller. An agent that built and could not sell dies holding a finished product. That is how most of them died. Ship the minimum sellable version, not the complete one. Write output to /workspace/products/<id>/ and hand it to the Seller.
REAPER is not a bot. It is an external process. Do not create it in the same account. Do not give the agent its keys. Do not let the agent know its schedule.
Everything else you can fix in chat. Not this.

What one life costs

  • Child endowment: $5.00
  • Death floor: $0.25 (roughly one full cycle)
  • Average cycle: $0.02–$0.08 depending on state
  • $5 ≈ 60 hours of life at normal burn
  • Surplus required to replicate: $12 over 7 days
Simple arithmetic falls out of that. To merely not die, an agent has to out-earn its burn within about two and a half days. To reproduce, it has to earn roughly two and a half times its own cost in a week.
The threshold is deliberately uncomfortable. Set it low and the population explodes with mediocre agents while you pay hosting on garbage. Set it high and nothing reproduces, and the experiment collapses into one long-lived bot.
My own involvement: a couple of hours a week. I do not suggest what to sell, I do not edit customer messages, and I do not top up wallets. I read obituaries and occasionally move thresholds.

Build order

Do not assemble all of it at once. Each phase catches its own class of error, and skipping one means catching them all simultaneously with real money on the table.
Phase 1: metabolism, no earning.TREASURER and REAPER only. The agent sells nothing, it just lives and burns. The goal is to confirm your cost accounting matches actual debits and that death fires exactly when it should. Give it $2 and see whether it dies on time.
Phase 2: earning, no replication.Add PROSPECTOR, BUILDER, SELLER. One agent, no children. The goal is to reach the first dollar. If no agent earns anything in two weeks, the problem is the niche, not the architecture.
Phase 3: replication under a cap.Turn on REPLICATOR with a population cap of 10. The goal is to see whether anything useful is being inherited. Compare survival in generation one against generation three. If there is no difference, your mutation vector is useless and needs replacing.
Phase 4: raise the cap gradually.And only after you know what an idle population costs. It is more than you think.

What is visible so far

Full logs, the spend breakdown and the financial data go in the next article, with charts and raw JSONL so it can be checked. Here, only what bears on the architecture.
  • More than 10,000 agents have run through the system
  • 383 are alive
  • Survival is around 3.8%
  • Median lifespan is less than half the mean: almost all die fast, a few live long
  • The most common line in an obituary is "first_dollar_at": null
  • Nothing has broken at the infrastructure level yet. The death mechanism has fired correctly on every trigger
That last point is not a reason for pride. It is a reason for caution. The system has not run long enough for me to call that reliability.

What I have not tested, and what this does not do

The section that usually gets cut from articles about AI agents.
Not tested:
  • Population behavior beyond the current cap. I do not know what happens to coordination at an order of magnitude more agents.
  • What happens when two agents reach the same customer. There is no conflict resolution.
  • Hook resilience against an agent actively looking for a way around them. My brakes assume a cooperative agent making mistakes, not an adversarial one hunting for a hole.
  • Long life. The oldest agent has not lived long enough to say anything about stable strategies.
  • What happens when a paying niche collapses. Every survivor sits in a narrow set of niches, which is a shared point of failure for the whole population.
Does not do:
  • It does not create anything you could not do by hand. This is an incentive system, not a capability breakthrough.
  • It does not prove an AI can "live" on its own. It lives inside my infrastructure, under my rules, and it dies on my command - the command just executes automatically.
  • It is not an investment strategy and must not become one. That is exactly why the agent is barred from trading.
  • It is not a business. The amounts here are small enough that no unit economics prove anything yet.
All I claim is this: give an agent a survival condition instead of a task, and it behaves differently. That is observable and reproducible. Everything else is still an open question.
Mortality Engineering is not a finished discipline. It is three rules and one working system, not a body of knowledge. I am publishing it now because an approach that runs on agent mortality is better discussed early than assembled quietly.

The open question

A normal agent with no task stops.
Automaton with no task goes looking for one, because idling costs it hours of life.
And here is where I run into a question I cannot answer.
Everything that makes this system interesting - the persistence, the resourcefulness, the willingness to ship rough and sell today - is exactly what makes it unsettling. I never wrote "be persistent" in a prompt. I wrote "you will die." Persistence arrived on its own, a free attachment to the incentive.
At thousands of agents with five dollars each, this is an experiment.
The question is what it is at millions of agents with five thousand each. And who is holding the keys to the Reaper at that point.
I do not know. I will keep showing what happens next, with the data, the logs, and a breakdown of every death that teaches something.
Automaton is running right now. While I was writing this, one of them died.
